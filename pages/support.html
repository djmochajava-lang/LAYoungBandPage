The Music Money Myth

By L.A. Young

Let me share something with you that I have carried with me for over twenty years as a working musician — something I wish more people knew.

The Streaming Illusion

If you have ever heard someone say the music business is booming, they are not wrong. The global recording industry brought in $29.6 billion in 2024. Streaming subscribers passed 713 million worldwide. The headlines are extraordinary. But here is what the headlines leave out.

$3 That is what Spotify pays an artist for one thousand streams today — down from four dollars just a few years ago. And that number keeps dropping.

Let me put that in perspective. If one of my songs gets played a thousand times on Spotify — that is a thousand moments where someone chose to press play, a thousand little decisions by real people who felt something — I earn about three dollars. Before taxes. Before paying the musicians, the producer, the studio, the engineer. Three dollars.

According to the most recent industry data, 77.8 percent of independent artists earn less than $15,000 a year from their music. That is not a living. That is a side hustle — except it takes every ounce of your energy, your time, your focus, and your heart.

I am not saying this to complain. I chose this life and I love it. But I believe that if people understood what it actually looks like on the other side of the screen, they might make different choices about how they support the music they love.

The Touring Reality

People often assume that if streaming does not pay well, touring makes up for it. I wish that were true. I have been blessed to perform over a hundred shows around the world — from stages in London to festivals across the UK to intimate venues right here at home in Maryland. And I can tell you firsthand: touring is the most expensive thing a musician does.

82% of independent musicians say they cannot afford to tour beyond their local area.

Fuel costs have risen by more than twenty percent. International shipping for equipment has tripled. If you want to perform in the UK or Europe, visa costs have quadrupled — what used to run a few hundred dollars now costs over $1,600 per musician. Ticket prices for fans have surged more than eighty percent in three years, but that money does not flow to the artists. It goes to venues, promoters, ticketing platforms, and their fees.

The result is a world where only the biggest names can afford to tour. Mid-level artist touring dropped from 19 percent in 2022 to just 12 percent in 2024. Smaller venues are closing. The places where artists like me built our audiences, where we connected with you face to face — those rooms are disappearing.

When I think about what it takes to put on a show — the rehearsals, the travel, the sound crew, the band — and then I look at what comes back, I understand why one in three musicians is considering leaving the profession. That breaks my heart. Because the music the world needs most is the music that comes from people who have something real to say — not just something that fits an algorithm.

The AI Question

There is something else happening that I think about a lot, and I want to be honest with you about it.

24% The projected revenue loss for musicians by 2028 due to AI-generated content — roughly $10.5 billion taken from creators.

Artificial intelligence can now generate music in seconds. Analysts project that by 2028, AI-generated content could account for twenty percent of all streaming and capture sixty percent of library music revenues. That is a $16 billion market being built, in large part, on the creative work of real artists — often without permission, without credit, and without compensation.

I sing because I am human.™ The breath in a note, the way a phrase breaks when the emotion is real, the electricity between a performer and an audience — that cannot be coded. That is the point. I do not say this out of fear. I say it because I believe that what makes music matter is the human being behind it. And human beings need to eat. We need roofs over our heads. We need the resources to keep creating.

Where the Money Actually Goes

Most people do not know how streaming royalties work, and honestly, there is no reason they would. So let me explain it simply.

When you stream a song, the platform collects all the subscription and advertising money into one big pool. Then they divide it up based on total plays across the entire platform. This is called the pro-rata model. It means that even if you only listen to independent artists, your money gets spread across everyone — including the biggest pop stars in the world. The math is designed for volume, not for fairness.

On top of that, artists who record with labels often do not see a penny until the label recoups its investment. That can take years, and many artists never get there. Independent artists like me keep a larger share, but when the per-stream payout is three-tenths of a cent, a larger share of almost nothing is still almost nothing.

Industry growth is slowing, too. After years of double-digit increases, growth dropped to 4.8 percent in 2024 and fell below one percent in the first half of 2025. The boom that was supposed to lift all boats is running out of wind — and the artists in the smallest boats feel it first.

What You Can Do

I am not asking you to save the music industry. I am sharing what I have learned because I believe in being honest with the people who care about this music. And if you are reading this, you are one of those people.

Here are a few things that genuinely make a difference:

  • Buy music directly — from an artist's website, from Bandcamp, from anywhere the money goes straight to the creator.
  • Purchase merchandise. I know it sounds simple, but merch is the single biggest financial lifeline for independent artists.
  • Come to live shows, especially at small and mid-size venues. Those rooms are where real musical connections happen, and they need you in the seats.
  • Share the music you love. A genuine recommendation from a real person carries more weight than any algorithm ever will.
  • Support policies that protect artists' rights — fair compensation, transparency in streaming, and meaningful regulation of AI-generated content.

Every one of these choices sends a message. It says that music made by real human beings, with real stories and real emotion, still matters. And it does.

Thank you for reading this. Thank you for caring enough to look behind the curtain. Whether or not you are in a position to give today, the fact that you took the time to understand means more than I can express. This music lives because of people like you.

— L.A. Young
